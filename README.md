## The costs live in the payment rail, not the coin

Two people can buy $500 of Bitcoin on the same exchange on the same day and pay very different amounts for it. The difference is almost never the coin. It's whether they used a credit card, a P2P seller, a bank transfer, or crypto they already owned somewhere else.

That's the decision worth slowing down for. Picking Bitcoin over Ethereum changes your risk profile. Picking the wrong payment route changes how much of your money actually converts into crypto, and that part is knowable before you buy.

## Step 2: Choose how you pay

Gate supports several fiat routes, and they behave differently enough that the choice matters more than which coin you pick first.

The card row deserves its own paragraph, because the advertised number and the billed number are rarely the same. Gate's card guide puts the platform-side fee from 0.08%, and notes that the issuing bank may add 2–5% on top, with currency conversion costs where your card currency differs from the purchase currency. On a $1,000 purchase, that guide's own example lands the total at $1,020–$1,050.

A more recent Gate guide is blunter: the cards that work, the limits, the fees and the exchange rate all depend on the payment provider and your region, so there is no single "Gate card fee." The number that matters is the quote on the confirmation screen before you hit pay. Read it. The amount of crypto you receive, not the headline percentage, is the real measure of what you paid.

P2P works differently in a way beginners often miss. You aren't buying from Gate's order book, you're buying from another person, with Gate holding the seller's crypto in escrow and releasing it once the seller confirms your payment arrived. Payment options on the P2P side are broader than the card route, covering bank transfers, PayPal, Apple Pay, Wise, Revolut and various region-specific services. Bank transfers often settle within a few hours on business days; digital payment services are usually instant.

If you want the smoothest first buy and don't mind paying for convenience, a debit card is the least fuss. If cost is the priority and you're comfortable reading seller terms, P2P is cheaper on paper. What you're trading for that saving is time and attention.

## Step 3: Place your first order

Within Gate's spot market you'll see two order types that matter as a beginner.

A **market order** fills immediately at whatever price the book offers. Fast, simple, slightly more expensive, because you're taking liquidity rather than providing it. A **limit order** sits at the price you name and only fills if the market reaches it. You pay less when it fills, and you may wait a long time or never fill at all.

Gate's spot fee structure at the entry tier is 0.10% for both maker and taker orders, dropping to 0.09% if you pay fees in GT, the platform token. Concretely: a $500 market buy costs about $0.50 in fees. Not the thing that will ruin your month. The card route and your bank are far bigger line items on a small first purchase.

## After the purchase: exchange wallet or your own

Leaving crypto on an exchange is convenient and, for small amounts you plan to trade, sensible. You can sell in seconds, and you're not managing keys.

The tradeoff is custody. On an exchange, you hold an IOU backed by the platform's security and solvency, not keys you control. Exchange-wallet crypto also sits in a hot-wallet environment by design, because the platform needs liquidity to serve withdrawals and trades.

The usual split people use: short-term trading balance stays on the exchange, long-term holdings go to a wallet you control, ideally a hardware one, with the seed phrase written down offline. Withdrawal fees are charged when you move assets out, and they vary by network, so it's worth checking the fee before moving a small balance that doesn't justify it.

## Beginner mistakes that cost actual money

**Buying with a credit card you won't pay off this month.** Several issuers classify crypto purchases as cash advances, which means interest from day one plus a fee. A debit card avoids most of this.

**Ignoring the quote screen.** The amount of crypto shown before you confirm is the honest number. Compare it to the market price and you'll see your real all-in cost.

**Assuming the volume task is easy.** Reward challenges are calibrated so the average user doesn't max them out. If the reward requires more trading than you'd otherwise do, it isn't free money.

**Skipping 2FA because verification already felt like a lot of work.** Verification protects the platform from fraud. 2FA protects you from someone with your password.

**Treating a limit order as a purchase.** It's an instruction, not a completed trade. If the price never reaches your number, nothing happens and no crypto appears.

**Sending crypto on the wrong network.** Deposits have to match the network the platform supports. A mismatch can mean permanently lost funds, and no support ticket fixes that.
